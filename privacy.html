Privacy

DesktopWiki is made by EmuKey LLC. This page says what the DesktopWiki app and this website collect. The full terms are in the license you accept when you install it.

Your wiki stays on your PC

Your wiki (pages, history, attachments, accounts, and vaults, which are encrypted in the browser before they reach it) is one file on the PC that runs DesktopWiki, with its backups in the folder you choose. DesktopWiki serves it only to the devices that connect to it on your network. It never sends your pages, attachments, accounts or any other content to EmuKey LLC or anyone else, unless you connect an AI app yourself (see AI agents below). Apart from the anonymous usage statistics below, it has no analytics, telemetry, advertising or crash reporting.

Update checks

DesktopWiki asks GitHub (api.github.com) whether a newer version is available when it starts and every few hours (and once a night, if an administrator has turned on automatic updates), and reads a small public file on GitHub (raw.githubusercontent.com) that says whether the beta is still running. It downloads an update only when an administrator chooses to install it, or by itself at night when automatic updates are on, and installs only one signed by EmuKey LLC. GitHub receives your IP address and the app’s version number, and handles them under the GitHub Privacy Statement.

A desktopwiki.net address

Only if an administrator chooses Configuration → A desktopwiki.net address → Get a desktopwiki.net address, DesktopWiki asks EmuKey’s names service (api.desktopwiki.net) for a random name, such as k7f2q9.desktopwiki.net, that leads to the PC’s address on your network (a private address such as 192.168.1.20, which can’t be reached from the internet). It sends that address, and again whenever it changes, and briefly sets a record that proves the name to Let’s Encrypt. The service keeps the name, a hash of a secret only your wiki knows, the address, and when the name was made and last heard from, and removes names unseen for 180 days. It doesn’t keep your IP address: to limit new names per network, it counts them under a salted hash of it that changes every day. It keeps no logs of requests. DesktopWiki then gets the name’s certificate from Let’s Encrypt (acme-v02.api.letsencrypt.org), which receives the name and your IP address under the Let’s Encrypt privacy policy. Both the name and the private address it leads to are public: they’re in DNS, and the name is in the public Certificate Transparency logs that list every certificate. Nothing about your wiki’s content is sent. Stop using this address removes the name and its records at once.

Away from home

Only if an administrator turns it on in Configuration, under Network. Away from home gives the wiki a second name, such as k7f2q9-away.desktopwiki.net, that leads to the PC’s address in Tailscale, a private network app (an address such as 100.101.102.103, which can’t be reached from the internet). The wiki sends the names service that address, and again whenever it changes. Open to the internet gives the wiki a public name, such as k7f2q9.away.desktopwiki.net, that leads to your home’s internet address. The names service takes that address from the wiki’s own request, not from anything the wiki says, and doesn’t keep it anywhere but in DNS. Your home’s internet address is then public: anyone can look the name up. To check that the wiki can be reached, the names service connects to that address on the wiki’s port, and the wiki answers with a random code it sent for that check. Both names are in DNS and in the public Certificate Transparency logs, like the first one. Turning either off removes its records at once.

Password health

When someone opens Password health in their vaults, their browser checks each password against Have I Been Pwned’s Pwned Passwords service (api.pwnedpasswords.com). It sends only the first five characters of the password’s SHA-1 hash, padded so the size of the answer gives nothing away, and compares the answer itself: the password, the rest of its hash and the item never leave the browser. The service receives your IP address; see the Have I Been Pwned privacy policy. An administrator can turn this off in Configuration → Vaults, and then nothing is sent. The list of sites that offer two-step sign-in comes with DesktopWiki, so checking it sends nothing.

Notifications

When someone turns on notifications in a browser or on a phone under Your account → Notifications, the wiki sends each notification to that browser through its maker’s push service: Google’s for Chrome and Android, Mozilla’s for Firefox, Apple’s for Safari, iPhone and iPad, or Microsoft’s for Edge. Each notification (the wiki’s name, who did what on which page, and the start of a comment or a share’s note) is encrypted for that one browser before it leaves your PC, so the push service can’t read it. The push service sees when a notification is sent and to which browser, receives your home’s IP address, and keeps an undelivered one for at most 12 hours; see its maker’s privacy policy. Nothing goes to EmuKey LLC. An administrator can turn browser notifications off for the whole wiki in Configuration → Notifications, which removes every browser. Windows notifications from the tray icon on the wiki’s PC don’t use a push service: they never leave the PC.

AI agents

If you make an AI key under Your account → AI agents and connect an AI app with it, such as Claude Code, DesktopWiki lets that app read what you can see in the wiki, apart from vaults and the spaces you keep from it. What the app does with what it reads is up to the app’s maker and their privacy policy. Nothing is shared this way unless someone makes a key, and an administrator can turn AI agents off for the whole wiki in Configuration → AI agents.

Usage statistics

To count how many people use DesktopWiki, once a day while it runs it sends EmuKey a short report with:

  • a random install ID, made on your PC the first time (it identifies that installation, not you);
  • the version of DesktopWiki;
  • the version of Windows;
  • whether it’s a new installation.

It never contains your wiki’s name, pages, files, accounts, names, email addresses or your computer’s name. We use the reports only as totals: how many installations there are, how many are in use, and which versions of DesktopWiki and Windows they run. The reports go to a counter we run on Cloudflare; your IP address reaches Cloudflare to deliver the report but isn’t stored with it. We keep reports for up to 400 days and never sell or share them.

To turn them off, an administrator clears Send anonymous usage statistics in Configuration → Usage statistics.

Help and feedback

When you send a message from Help and feedback in your wiki’s profile menu, DesktopWiki sends EmuKey what you wrote, the email address you gave for the answer (if any), the versions of DesktopWiki and Windows, and your language. A picture goes too if you attach one, and an administrator can choose to attach the wiki’s log, which names people and spaces but holds no page text; they can read it on that page first. Nothing else from your wiki is sent. The message passes through a service we run on Cloudflare, which keeps nothing and forwards it to our support inbox as an email. We use it only to answer you and improve DesktopWiki, and never sell or share it.

This website

DesktopWiki.com counts its visits with Cloudflare Web Analytics, to learn how many people visit, which pages they read, where they came from and how many download the app. For each page view it records the page, the site that linked to it, the browser, operating system and type of device, and the country. It sets no cookies and stores nothing in your browser, and Cloudflare says it doesn’t fingerprint visitors or use IP addresses to follow them. We see only totals, never individual visitors. When you click Download, the link also passes through a download counter we run on Cloudflare, which counts each person once a day: it keeps a one-way scramble of your IP address and browser made with a secret that changes every day, so it can’t be turned back into your IP address or matched with another day, and your IP address itself isn’t stored. See the Cloudflare Privacy Policy. The site is hosted on GitHub Pages, which receives your IP address to serve it.
